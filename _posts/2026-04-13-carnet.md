When building an intelligent system to identify real-world objects, the default instinct is often to reach for standard image classification. But what happens when your catalog contains thousands—or tens of thousands—of distinct reference numbers? What happens when new vehicle models are introduced daily, and waiting hours for a neural network to retrain is out of the question?

This exact challenge led to the development of **CarNet**, a scalable, embedding-based image retrieval pipeline tailored for deep, high-cardinality catalogs like the Stanford Cars Dataset.

Scaling search beyond traditional boundaries means rethinking how a model "understands" an image. In this case study, we'll break down the architecture, training methodologies, and deployment strategies of CarNet.

---

## 1. The Paradigm Shift: From Classification to Retrieval

Traditional multi-class neural networks (where $N$ equals your number of classes) suffer from a major operational bottleneck: **every time a new class is introduced, the model must be modified and retrained.** For an active catalog of vehicles, this is fundamentally unscalable.

Drawing inspiration from industry patterns in *Content-based Image Retrieval* and *Image Search Engines*, CarNet abandons classification entirely. Instead, we treat the problem through the lens of **Metric Learning** and **Embedding-based Retrieval**. 

1. **Feature Extraction:** A deep neural network acts as an encoder mapping each car image into a continuous metric space (a 512-dimensional vector).
2. **Distance as Similarity:** Instead of predicting a specific car, the network is optimized to pull embeddings of the *same* car closer together, while pushing embeddings of *different* cars further apart.
3. **Retrieval inference:** We encode a query image into its 512-D vector and perform an exact mathematical nearest-neighbor search against a pre-computed "gallery". 

No retraining. If a new car model hits the market, we simply run its canonical images through our encoder and append the new 512-D vectors to our gallery.

## 2. Choosing the Core Engine

Our early baselines utilizing CNNs like `ResNet50` gave us a functional start, but identifying fine-grained visual features (like the subtle difference between a 2010 and 2012 model) requires superior representational capacity. 

We pivoted to Vision Transformers (ViTs)—specifically `deit_small_distilled_patch16_224` (Data-efficient Image Transformers). It offered the best trade-off in our benchmarks, outperforming larger base models and our CNN baselines while maintaining a lightweight footprint of just 22 million parameters.

Attached to this backbone is a simplified projection head (`Linear -> BatchNorm`), ensuring all feature vectors output as size 512 and are strictly **L2-normalized**.

## 3. Training the Metric Space

Metric learning lives and dies by its loss function. While building CarNet, we evaluated two distinct approaches to learning deep metric embeddings:

*   **Classification-based / Stateful methods:** (e.g., ArcFace) which learn discriminative embeddings using class prediction weights.
*   **Contrastive / Stateless methods:** (e.g., TripletMargin) which directly optimize the relative mathematical Euclidean distances between pairs or triplets of embeddings in batches.

Our empirical results showed that contrastive losses—specifically **BatchHardTriplet** and **TripletMargin**—provided the most stable convergence and yielded highest exact-match accuracy (`Recall@1`). 

### Solving the Long-Tail Problem
Real-world datasets are messy. Popular car models have thousands of photos, while rare models have very few. To prevent the network from collapsing into a biased state where it only accurately maps frequent classes, we implemented an `MPerClassSampler`. During training, batches are carefully curated to contain exactly $P$ distinct classes, with $K$ instances per class. This guarantees our Triplet loss always has balanced positive and negative pairs to learn from.

## 4. Engineering the Inference Pipeline 

Getting a model to perform well in a Jupyter Notebook is only half the battle. A truly scalable solution needs a robust deployment pipeline.

We wrapped CarNet in a **FastAPI** service with a secondary **Streamlit** user interface. 

### The Dynamic Index Management
Rather than leaning on heavy Approximate Nearest Neighbor (ANN) libraries like FAISS, which add needless infrastructural overhead for small to medium sets, we leveraged the power of **Exact Cosine Similarity**.

Given a gallery size of around 10k-20k classes and our compact 512-D embeddings, an exact matrix dot-product takes less than 1 millisecond on a modern CPU. 

*   **Indexing (/index):** Fast automated generation of the inference gallery index. Point it at a directory of new car folders, and it processes every image, writing a self-contained `gallery_index.npz` to the disk.
*   **Searching (/identify):** Drop an image via a REST client or the Streamlit web UI. The server ingests the PIL image, mathematically derives the embedding, and returns a JSON payload of the unified top candidates sorted by confidence.

## Conclusion

CarNet highlights an important lesson in modern deep learning system design: **sometimes the best way to classify something is not to classify it at all.**

By leaning into vector embeddings and metric learning, we escaped the scaling constraints of Softmax. The result is a fast, highly-performant, and natively extensible architectural pattern that easily accepts new data without endless retraining cycles—a vital design pattern for any engineering team building image-search applications today.
